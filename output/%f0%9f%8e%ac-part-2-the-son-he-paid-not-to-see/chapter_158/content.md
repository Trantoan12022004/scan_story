Michael stared at his father.

“What symptoms?”

The old man looked away.

“Michael—”

“What symptoms?”

His voice cracked this time.

Not like a doctor.

Like a son.

The father sighed.

“Pain.”

“How long?”

“A while.”

“How long is a while?”

“Six months.”

Michael stepped backward.

“Six months?”

His father nodded.

He had been getting weaker.

Losing weight.

Sleeping badly.

Some mornings, his hands shook so badly he needed both of them just to hold a coffee cup.

But every time Michael called, his father said the same thing.

I’m fine.

Busy.

Getting old.

Nothing worth worrying about.

Michael covered his mouth.

“You hid this from me.”

“I didn’t want you diagnosing your own father over the phone.”

“You didn’t even give me the chance to help.”

The old man looked at him.

“That was the point.”

Michael stared.

His father’s voice softened.

“When your mother got sick, you stopped being her son.”

Michael flinched.

“You became her doctor.”

Silence.

Years earlier, Michael had been a young resident when his mother became seriously ill.

He read every scan.

Every lab.

Every paper.

Every possible treatment.

He sat beside her bed analyzing numbers while she kept asking him one thing.

“Can you just sit with me?”

After she died, Michael never forgave himself for spending her final weeks trying to solve something instead of simply being her son.

His father remembered.

That was why he had hidden the tests.

“I wasn’t going to put you through that again.”

Michael’s eyes filled.

“You don’t get to decide that for me.”

“I know.”

The father’s voice broke slightly.

“But I was scared.”

Michael looked down.

That confession hurt more than anything else.

His father had never admitted fear.

Not when the factory closed.

Not when they lost their house.

Not when his wife died.

Not once.

Michael quietly asked,

“Have you seen the results?”

“No.”

“Why come alone?”

The older man gave a tired smile.

“I thought if it was bad, I’d have ten minutes to be afraid by myself before I told you.”

Michael closed his eyes.

The receptionist stood completely still behind the desk.

Minutes earlier, she had seen a tired, poorly dressed old man.

Now she was watching a father try to protect his son from his own possible diagnosis.

Michael opened his eyes.

“Come with me.”

His father shook his head.

“I can walk.”

“I know.”

Michael didn’t let go of his arm.

“I’m still walking with you.”

They moved toward a consultation room.

Then the receptionist finally spoke.

“Sir.”

The older man turned.

Her aggression was gone.

“I’m sorry.”

He studied her.

She swallowed.

“I shouldn’t have spoken to you like that.”

The old man nodded once.

Then followed his son through the door.

Inside, Michael did something difficult.

He did not open the chart first.

He sat beside his father.

“What do you want?”

His father looked confused.

“What?”

“Right now.”

Michael took a breath.

“Do you want me to be your doctor or your son?”

The older man’s eyes filled.

“My son.”

Michael nodded.

“Then we’ll have another doctor explain it.”

His father reached across the table.

For the first time in years, they held hands.

A physician who had reviewed the testing entered a few minutes later.

She sat across from them.

No dramatic promises.

No certainty where there wasn’t any.

She explained that the scans showed something abnormal.

Something that needed further testing.

A biopsy.

More imaging.

More answers before anyone could say exactly what it meant.

Michael’s grip tightened around his father’s hand.

His father whispered,

“So you don’t know if it’s cancer.”

“Not yet.”

The older man exhaled.

Not relief.

Not exactly.

But the terror of the unknown became slightly more bearable once it had honest words around it.

Michael looked at him.

“We’ll deal with whatever it is.”

His father gave him a tired look.

“We?”

“Yes.”

“I thought I asked for my son.”

Michael’s eyes filled again.

“You did.”

He squeezed his father’s hand.

“And your son isn’t going anywhere.”

The old man smiled.

Then the physician looked back at the chart.

“There is something else.”

Both men turned.

She explained that one result did not fit the pattern they had expected.

Michael immediately tensed.

“What result?”

The physician hesitated.

“His blood type.”

The old man frowned.

“So?”

Michael stared at the page.

Then his face changed.

He knew his own blood type.

He knew his mother’s.

And he knew what his father had always told him his was.

The numbers didn’t fit.

His father noticed.

“What?”

Michael looked up slowly.

“Dad…”

The older man’s expression sharpened.

“What is it?”

Michael shook his head.

“Probably nothing. Records can be wrong. We verify before assuming anything.”

But the father was watching him too closely.

“What are you not saying?”

Michael hesitated.

Then turned the chart toward him.

“We need to repeat this test.”

“Why?”

Michael’s voice dropped.

“Because if this result is correct…”

He stopped.

The father looked at him.

“Say it.”

Michael swallowed.

“It means one of the things we’ve believed about our family for forty years may not be true.”

The room went silent.

The old man stared at the paper.

Then something unexpected happened.

He smiled.

A sad, exhausted smile.

Michael frowned.

“Dad?”

His father leaned back.

“You finally found it.”

Michael’s blood ran cold.

“Found what?”

The old man closed his eyes.

“The reason your mother made me promise never to let you see my old medical records.”

Michael stared at him.

The frightening scan suddenly wasn’t the only secret in the room.

His father opened his eyes again.

And whispered,

“Michael… I raised you.”

A pause.

“I loved you from the first night I held you.”

Michael’s face collapsed.

“But I was never the man whose blood you inherited.”
