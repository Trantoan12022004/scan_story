The biker stopped speaking.

He stared at the child as if one word had just torn open twenty years of his life.

Rose.

His younger sister.

The girl he had searched for until there were no new places left to search.

The sister everyone eventually told him to stop waiting for.

He looked at the child again.

Same grey eyes.

Same small dimple near the mouth.

Even the way she clenched her jaw when frightened reminded him of Rose as a teenager.

His voice came out rough.

“Where is your mother?”

The girl shook her head quickly.

“I don’t know.”

Behind them, the young man in denim stopped several feet from the booth.

The girl recoiled.

“That’s him.”

The biker looked at the man.

The man raised both hands slightly.

“She’s confused.”

The little girl grabbed the wolf patch again.

“No.”

Her voice cracked.

“He told me Mom left.”

The biker stood.

Not aggressively.

Just enough to make himself impossible to ignore.

The man in denim stopped moving.

The biker looked down at the girl.

“What did Rose tell you?”

She reached into the pocket of her muddy jacket.

Her fingers pulled out a tiny folded photograph.

In it, a much younger version of the biker stood beside a smiling teenage girl.

Rose.

Both of them wore matching wolf patches sewn onto cheap denim jackets.

The biker’s face collapsed.

He took the photograph with shaking fingers.

On the back, Rose had written one sentence:

If she ever needs help, she’ll know the wolf.

He closed his eyes.

All those years, Rose had remembered him.

The little girl started crying harder.

“She said you were her brother.”

He crouched in front of her.

This time his eyes were wet.

“I am.”

The girl stared at him.

“Then you’re my uncle?”

He nodded.

And something in her finally broke.

She leaned into him.

He wrapped both arms around her, careful and protective, while she sobbed into the old leather vest.

The man in denim took one step backward.

The biker noticed.

“Where’s Rose?”

No answer.

The girl lifted her face from his chest.

“She tried to leave last night.”

The biker went still.

The young man turned toward the door.

But several people in the diner had already noticed the fear on the child’s face.

Nobody touched him.

Nobody threatened him.

They simply watched.

The biker kept one arm around his niece.

Then looked at the man who had walked in expecting to take her away without questions.

His voice stayed calm.

“You told her Rose left.”

The man said nothing.

The girl whispered,

“Mom told me something else before I ran.”

Her uncle looked down.

“What?”

She pressed her small hand against the wolf patch again.

Her voice trembled.

“She said if I found you… it meant she couldn’t come herself.”

And for the first time, the biker understood why his sister had taught her daughter to recognize a patch from a life she had supposedly left behind.
