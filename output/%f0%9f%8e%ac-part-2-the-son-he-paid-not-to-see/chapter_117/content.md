The woman covered her mouth.

For a moment, she could only stare.

The girl stood perfectly still, tears sliding through the dirt on her cheeks, like she was waiting to be told she had come to the wrong person.

But the woman already knew.

She knew from the photograph.

From the watch.

From the note.

And from that tiny mark under the girl’s ear.

The same mark she had kissed a thousand times in the first six weeks of her baby’s life.

The same mark the doctors told her she would never see again.

“What’s your name, sweetheart?”

The girl’s lower lip trembled.

“Rosie.”

The woman nodded like the name had hit her in the chest.

“And your mom?”

The girl wiped her cheek with the back of her hand.

“She said her name was Mara. She said if anything ever happened, I had to find the lady in the watch.”

The woman sat down slowly because her legs felt weak.

Mara.

The nurse.

The only one who had shown her kindness the night everything was taken from her.

Years ago, the woman had been young, unmarried, and from a family that cared more about reputation than love.

When she gave birth, they told her the baby was too weak.

They told her there had been complications.

They told her not to ask to see the body.

She screamed. She begged. But they sedated her, signed papers over her tears, and buried the truth under money and silence.

Only Mara had cried with her.

Only Mara had whispered, “I’m so sorry.”

Now the child standing beside her table looked exactly like the little face she had mourned.

The woman’s eyes filled.

“Where is your mom now?”

Rosie shook her head.

Her voice came out small and broken.

“She got sick.”

The woman closed her eyes.

“When?”

“Three days ago.”

That nearly destroyed her.

Mara had kept the secret all these years.

Raised the child.

Protected her.

And in the end, sent her back.

The woman looked at the note again. The handwriting was shaky, rushed.

If she looks like me, she’s yours.

Not I think.

Not maybe.

She’s yours.

Rosie took one tiny step closer.

“She said you didn’t leave me.”

The woman looked up sharply.

Fresh tears spilled down the girl’s face.

“She said they made you lose me.”

The woman broke then.

Not loudly.

Not dramatically.

Just one sharp breath that turned into a sob she couldn’t stop.

She reached for the girl very slowly.

Rosie hesitated.

The woman’s hand stopped in the air.

“I won’t make you come to me,” she whispered. “Only if you want to.”

Rosie stared at her for one long second.

Then stepped forward.

When the woman wrapped her arms around her, she shook so hard she could barely hold on.

Rosie held her back just as tightly.

The watch chain pressed between them.

The woman buried her face in the child’s messy hair and cried for the baby she had lost, the years she had missed, and the stranger who had just become her whole world again.

Then Rosie whispered the one thing that shattered whatever was left of her.

“I was scared you wouldn’t know me.”

The woman pulled back just enough to look at her.

With tears streaming down her face, she touched the little birthmark under Rosie’s ear.

And whispered,

“I knew you the second I opened the watch.”
