The man didn’t answer.

That silence frightened Lily more than the accusation.

She remained on the marble beside the music box, breathing hard from the few steps she had taken.

Leo stood across from her.

Bare feet.

Torn clothes.

Same birthmark.

Same eyes.

Lily looked at her father.

“Who is he?”

His lips moved.

Nothing came out.

Leo answered instead.

“Your brother.”

Lily stared at him.

Her father closed his eyes.

Not brother.

Twin.

They had been born minutes apart.

Lily and Leo.

Their mother had chosen the music box before they were born.

Two names engraved underneath.

One melody she played every night while holding them both.

Then their mother died when the children were still very young.

Afterward, everything changed.

Their father’s family controlled the estate.

Lily had medical needs and required constant care.

Leo did not.

And someone decided one child would be easier to raise than two.

The father had been told Leo was taken by relatives temporarily.

Then came papers saying the boy had been adopted elsewhere.

He signed them.

He told himself it was best.

He told Lily she had once had an imaginary friend because explaining the missing boy became harder every year.

But Lily remembered pieces.

A little hand inside hers.

A boy sleeping beside her during storms.

A rhythm tapped against her wrist.

And the music box.

Her father had hidden it beneath the wheelchair because he couldn’t bring himself to throw away the last thing carrying both names.

Leo looked at him.

“You never came.”

The man’s eyes filled.

“I thought you were safe.”

Leo’s expression barely changed.

“You never checked.”

That hurt more because it was true.

Years passed.

The family who had taken Leo disappeared from the city.

Then their money disappeared too.

By the time he was old enough to understand what had happened, there was nobody left to answer his questions.

Only one possession remained from his childhood.

A tiny photograph of two toddlers sitting beside a music box.

On the back were the names Lily and Leo.

He spent years looking for her.

Then one evening he saw a photograph online.

A wealthy family gala.

A girl in a wheelchair wearing pale pink.

Same face.

Same birthmark.

Lily.

So he came.

Not for money.

Not for revenge.

To see whether she remembered.

Lily touched the engraving again.

“Why did you put the music box under my chair?”

Her father finally spoke.

“I didn’t.”

Everyone went still.

Leo looked at him.

Lily did too.

The man stepped closer to the chair.

“I found it there after your mother died.”

His voice broke.

“I never knew who hid it.”

Lily looked at Leo.

Then at the box.

For years, each of them had believed the other had vanished completely.

But their mother had left one instruction beneath the object they both remembered.

Always find each other.

Lily reached toward Leo.

He hesitated only a second.

Then took her hand.

She looked at her father through tears.

“You told me I was alone.”

The man’s face collapsed.

Leo squeezed her fingers.

And for the first time since childhood, Lily finally had proof that the person she remembered beside her had never been imaginary.

He had been searching for her too.
