The older woman stood slowly.

“You went to Clara?”

The woman in green shook her head.

“She’s confused.”

The girl reached into her faded sweater.

“No, I’m not.”

She pulled out a folded envelope.

“My mama said to give you this if I found you.”

The older woman opened it.

The handwriting alone made her knees weaken.

Clara.

Her daughter.

The letter began:

Mom, if this reaches you, please know I never stopped trying to come home.

The older woman covered her mouth.

For twelve years, she had believed Clara wanted nothing to do with the family.

The woman in green had told her Clara had taken money and disappeared.

But the letter told a different story.

Clara had returned twice.

Both times, she had been turned away before reaching her mother.

The older woman looked up.

“You told me she refused to see me.”

The woman in green’s voice cracked.

“She was going to ruin everything.”

“What everything?”

Silence.

Then the little girl said,

“Mama said Grandpa left her something.”

The older woman went still.

Her late husband’s will.

Clara had been left forty percent of the family foundation.

If Clara returned, the woman in green’s husband would lose control of it.

The older woman stared at her.

“You kept my daughter away for money?”

“It wasn’t just money.”

“Then what?”

The woman finally broke.

“Clara knew the foundation accounts were being used to cover private debts.”

The room fell silent.

The older woman looked toward several board members standing among the guests.

Then back at the letter.

There was one final line.

If I don’t make it home, please protect Lily. She has nothing to do with what they did.

The older woman looked down at the child.

“Where is your mother now?”

Lily’s eyes filled.

“In the hospital.”

The older woman stopped breathing.

“She’s alive?”

Lily nodded.

“She told me she didn’t have enough money for another treatment.”

The older woman looked around the ballroom.

At the chandeliers.

The diamonds.

The champagne.

The thousands of dollars in white roses scattered across the floor.

Then at her granddaughter wearing shoes with worn soles.

She turned toward the woman in green.

“You knew Clara was sick?”

No answer.

That was answer enough.

The older woman took Lily’s hand.

“Get my car.”

The woman in green stepped forward.

“Please, don’t make a scene.”

The older woman looked at her.

“You made my daughter beg outside her own family’s door.”

Her voice stayed calm.

“This is not a scene.”

She picked up one of the fallen white roses and placed it back into Lily’s basket.

“This is the moment your lie ends.”

Lily looked up.

“Are we going to Mama?”

The older woman knelt and touched the crescent pendant.

“Yes.”

Her voice broke.

“And when we get there, you’re going to tell her something for me.”

“What?”

The older woman smiled through tears.

“Tell Clara her mother finally found the way home.”
