Her father stared at the woman.

“You knew?”

She immediately shook her head.

“She’s confused. She’s freezing.”

Emma grabbed her father’s hand.

“I’m not confused.”

Her legs buckled.

He caught her and gently lowered her back into the wheelchair.

Then Emma whispered,

“She makes me practice when you’re gone.”

The father’s expression changed.

“Practice what?”

“Standing.”

The woman stepped forward.

“I was helping her.”

Emma recoiled.

“No.”

That single word stopped him.

Emma looked at her father.

“She said if I told you I was getting stronger, you’d send me away.”

His face went blank.

“I would never say that.”

“I know now.”

The woman’s voice cracked.

“She misunderstood.”

Then Emma reached into the pocket of her soaked cardigan.

She pulled out a folded piece of paper.

“I took this from her room.”

Her father opened it.

It was from Emma’s physical therapist.

Three weeks earlier.

Progress significant. Patient has regained partial weight-bearing ability. Recommend supervised standing exercises with family present.

He read it twice.

Then looked at the woman.

“You told me the therapist said she was getting worse.”

Silence.

Emma began crying again.

“She canceled my appointments after that.”

Her father’s hands started shaking.

“Why?”

The woman looked away.

Because after Emma’s mother died, the woman had become her full-time caregiver.

And the father had trusted her completely.

But if Emma recovered enough to no longer need constant care…

the woman would lose the house she was living in.

The salary.

And the control she had built around their family.

“You kept my daughter afraid of her own legs?”

“I needed time.”

He stared at her.

“You used my child to buy yourself time.”

She started crying.

“I never meant for this to happen.”

Emma looked at the water running across the driveway.

Then quietly said,

“She sprayed me because I stood up this morning.”

Her father closed his eyes.

That was the moment everything became clear.

He took out his phone.

“Leave.”

The woman stared at him.

“Please—”

“Leave before the police get here.”

When she finally walked away, Emma looked at her father.

“Daddy?”

He turned.

“Do you think I can stand again?”

His eyes filled.

He knelt in front of her and held out both hands.

“I don’t know how far you can go.”

Emma placed her tiny hands in his.

“But nobody gets to make you afraid of trying again.”

And this time, when Emma rose from the wheelchair, her father was there to catch her.
