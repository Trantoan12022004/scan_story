The woman stared at the boy.

“Where did you get this?”

Her voice barely existed.

The boy looked at the bracelet in her hand.

“My dad kept it.”

She swallowed.

“Your father’s name is Adrian?”

The boy nodded.

The woman closed her eyes.

A tear rolled down her cheek.

Thirty years earlier, she had given birth to a baby boy.

Adrian.

He had the same crescent birthmark near his temple.

She remembered kissing it once.

Only once.

Then something went wrong.

Doctors rushed him away.

Hours later, her husband came into the room alone.

He told her their son had died.

She begged to see him.

He refused.

Said the baby had been too badly affected.

Said remembering him that way would only destroy her.

So she went home without her child.

There was a funeral.

A tiny closed casket.

A hospital bracelet placed inside a velvet box and given to her afterward.

She wore the family crest every day because it was the only piece of him she believed she still carried.

Until now.

The woman looked at the bracelet again.

“This was supposed to be buried with him.”

The boy’s expression changed.

“He knows.”

Her eyes snapped toward him.

“What?”

“My dad knows you thought he was dead.”

That sentence broke something inside her.

She gripped the armrest of her wheelchair.

“Then why didn’t he come?”

The boy looked down.

“Because he thought you gave him away.”

Silence.

The woman shook her head immediately.

“No.”

Her voice cracked.

“No, no…”

The boy’s eyes filled.

“He said his father told him you didn’t want a sick baby.”

She covered her mouth.

Her husband.

The man who had stood beside her at the funeral.

The man who held her while she cried.

The man who spent decades telling her to stop blaming herself.

Adrian had not died.

He had been taken.

And somewhere along the way, he had been told his mother chose to let him go.

The woman whispered,

“Where is he?”

The boy hesitated.

Then looked toward the floor-to-ceiling windows behind her.

Her breathing changed.

She slowly turned her wheelchair.

Outside, beyond the glass, stood a man in his early thirties.

He did not enter.

He simply stood in the sunset.

One hand resting against the window.

The woman stared at his face.

The same eyes.

The same jaw.

And beside his temple—

the same crescent mark.

Her hand flew to her mouth.

The boy whispered,

“He didn’t know if you’d want to see him.”

She began crying harder.

Thirty years of grief collapsed into one impossible moment.

The man outside still didn’t move.

He looked terrified.

Not of her.

Of being rejected again.

The woman lifted the old bracelet against her chest.

Then looked at the boy.

“Tell your father something for me.”

The boy waited.

She shook her head.

“No.”

Her hands moved to the wheels of her chair.

“I’ll tell him myself.”

She pushed herself toward the glass.

Slowly.

Trembling.

The man outside saw her coming.

His face collapsed.

And before the door even opened, she raised the hospital bracelet so he could see it.

Adrian covered his mouth.

Because for thirty years, he had believed his mother threw him away.

And for thirty years, she had been mourning a son who was still waiting to come home.
