The woman instinctively covered the necklace with her hand.

The man stared at her.

“Where did you get that?”

She forced a smile.

“It belonged to your mother.”

“No.”

The boy stood from the piano.

“My mother wore it in every photograph.”

The woman’s face tightened.

The man turned toward her.

“Claire…”

She began backing away.

“You told us Catherine died overseas.”

“She did.”

The boy shook his head.

“No.”

He opened his small cloth bag.

Inside were dozens of letters.

All addressed to the mansion.

All unopened.

“My mother wrote these until the year she died.”

The man took one.

His own name was written across the envelope.

His hands began trembling.

“She was alive?”

The boy nodded.

“She waited for someone to answer.”

Claire whispered,

“You don’t understand.”

The man looked at her.

“Then explain.”

Her composure finally broke.

Catherine had inherited half the estate.

But if she disappeared and remained legally presumed dead, control passed to her brother—and through him, eventually to Claire.

“So you hid her letters?”

Claire began crying.

“I was protecting this family.”

“You erased my sister.”

The boy lowered his eyes.

“She didn’t care about the money.”

The man looked at him.

“What did she want?”

The boy’s voice cracked.

“You.”

Silence.

“She said her brother was the only person who might believe her.”

The man covered his mouth.

Fifteen years of birthdays.

Christmases.

Searches that had ended too early.

A funeral without a body.

Then he looked at the child.

“How old are you?”

“Twelve.”

The answer hit him harder than anything else.

“You’re Catherine’s son.”

The boy nodded.

The man slowly knelt in front of him.

“Then I’m your uncle.”

The boy’s eyes filled.

“My mom said you played that song together when you were kids.”

The man started crying.

“She taught it to me.”

The boy shook his head gently.

“No.”

“She said you taught it to her.”

The man broke.

He pulled the boy into his arms.

Across the hall, Claire stood alone beneath the chandeliers.

Then the boy whispered,

“There’s one more thing.”

He handed his uncle the final letter.

It had been written only days before Catherine died.

At the bottom were seven words:

Please give my son the home I lost.

The man looked at his nephew.

Then toward the grand staircase.

“You’re not leaving tonight.”

The boy stared at him.

“Why?”

The man held Catherine’s ring between them.

“Because this was your mother’s home.”

His voice cracked.

“And now it’s yours too.”
