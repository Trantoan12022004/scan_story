The groom stared at his sister.

“What did you say?”

She was crying now.

“Our father.”

The baby had stopped screaming.

He lay against the girl’s chest, making tiny exhausted sounds.

The groom looked at him again.

Grey-green eyes.

Their father’s eyes.

The same unusual color both siblings had spent their childhood hearing strangers comment on.

The bride stepped backward.

That movement caught the groom’s attention.

He looked at her.

“You know whose baby this is.”

She shook her head.

But his sister interrupted.

“Yes, she does.”

The bride closed her eyes.

The groom’s sister walked toward the muddy girl.

Slowly.

Carefully.

“Where did you find him?”

“Behind the old chapel.”

The sister froze.

So did the bride.

The groom saw both reactions.

“What chapel?”

Nobody answered.

He stepped closer.

“What chapel?”

His sister finally looked at him.

“The one Dad used to visit every Sunday.”

Their father had died eight months earlier.

Officially, he had spent his final years alone after their mother’s death.

At least that was what his children believed.

But his daughter had discovered something while sorting through his belongings.

Letters.

Dozens of them.

All from the same woman.

A woman named Anna.

The letters went back nearly twenty years.

The groom stared at his sister.

“You never told me.”

“I didn’t understand them.”

Then she looked at the bride.

“But she did.”

The groom’s face changed.

His bride had helped his sister clean out their father’s study.

She had seen the letters.

She had read enough to learn that Anna had a daughter.

A daughter their father had quietly supported for years.

Nobody in the family knew.

Then, three weeks before the wedding, that daughter contacted the bride.

She was pregnant.

Alone.

And desperate to meet the family her mother had spent years hiding from her.

The groom looked at his bride.

“She contacted you?”

Tears spilled down her face.

“Yes.”

“And you never told me?”

“She was going to ruin everything.”

The words escaped before she could stop them.

The groom stared at her.

The ballroom went completely silent.

The bride began shaking.

She had spent a year planning the wedding.

The guest list included business partners, relatives, reporters, people whose opinions her family considered important.

Then suddenly there was a secret daughter.

A baby.

Questions about inheritance.

Questions about the groom’s father.

Questions she couldn’t control.

So she convinced the woman not to come.

Promised money.

Promised help.

Promised she would introduce everyone after the wedding.

But that morning, the woman appeared anyway.

Weak.

Frightened.

Carrying her newborn.

The bride met her at the old chapel before the ceremony.

They argued.

Then the woman became ill.

An ambulance was called.

In the confusion, the bride panicked.

She left the baby wrapped safely near the chapel entrance, believing someone nearby would immediately find him.

The muddy girl had.

The groom looked at her in disbelief.

“You left a newborn because you were afraid of a scandal?”

The bride covered her face.

“I thought someone would find him.”

“Someone did.”

His eyes moved toward the exhausted child who had carried the infant all the way into the ballroom.

A girl with dirt on her face had shown more courage than every wealthy adult in the room.

His sister stepped closer to the baby.

Then she noticed something around his tiny wrist.

A thin silver chain.

Her breath caught.

Attached to it was their father’s old wedding ring.

The ring that had disappeared after his funeral.

She touched it gently.

The groom saw.

His face collapsed.

Their father had not simply known Anna’s family.

He had known this baby was coming.

He had wanted the child connected to them.

The groom looked at his sister.

She whispered,

“Dad gave that ring to Mom.”

The muddy girl shifted the infant carefully.

“There was a note too.”

She reached inside her coat.

The bride went completely still.

The girl handed the folded paper to the groom.

He opened it.

His father’s handwriting.

Only one sentence.

If anything happens to me, my children deserve to know they have a sister.

The groom stopped breathing.

His sister covered her mouth.

The bride looked at the floor.

And suddenly the infant in the muddy girl’s arms was no longer a stranger who had interrupted a wedding.

He was their nephew.

The last living connection to a sister they had never been allowed to know.

The groom slowly approached the girl.

He didn’t take the baby immediately.

He looked at her first.

“You carried him here?”

She nodded.

“He wouldn’t stop crying.”

His voice broke.

“Thank you.”

Then his sister reached for the infant.

The girl carefully placed him in her arms.

The moment she looked down at his face, she began sobbing.

Those grey-green eyes opened again.

Their father’s eyes.

The groom stood beside her.

Neither sibling spoke.

They didn’t need to.

For eight months, they thought their father had taken his final secrets to the grave.

Instead, one of them had just arrived at a wedding—

crying in the arms of a stranger.
