By midnight, the forty-eighth-floor lab had been transformed from a scene of corporate despair into a high-octane mechanical forge.

Gone was the defeatist atmosphere. In its place was the frantic, rhythmic clatter of metal tools, the hiss of industrial torches, and the sharp, focused commands of a man who commanded absolute authority without ever raising his voice.

Paul Gregson stood beside the vacuum chamber, wiping sweat from his forehead as he handed Dean a specialized calibration gauge.

"The return line has been widened by two point five millimeters, exactly as you specified," Gregson panted, looking at Dean with a newfound, almost terrified respect. "We've recalibrated the injector phase-shift by twelve point four hertz. But... testing it under full load is going to take hours of simulation runs. We don't have hours."

"Simulations are for people who don't trust physics," Dean said, his hands moving with fluid, practiced precision as he tightened the final titanium bolt on the cooling manifold. "We aren't running a simulation. We're doing a live burn."

Gregson’s eyes widened in horror. "A live burn? Without a secondary containment simulation? If the acoustic resonance hasn't cleared, the entire core will rupture. We'll blow the roof off the building!"

"It won't rupture," Dean said calmly, standing up and wiping his greasy hands on a rag. "The math is clean. More importantly, the machine is telling the truth now."

Sloan Hutton stood on the upper observation platform, leaning against the glass railing with a cup of black coffee in her hand. She had discarded her blazer hours ago, her silk blouse sleeves rolled up to her elbows, watching Dean move among her elite engineers like a seasoned conductor leading an orchestra.

For the past four hours, she had watched him dismantle a multi-million-dollar propulsion failure that had baffled her best minds for a month. He didn't check manuals. He didn't consult software code. He worked by touch, by sound, and by an innate, terrifying mastery of mechanical engineering that belonged to a different era.

"Status?" Sloan called down from the platform.

Dean looked up at her, his face streaked with carbon dust, his eyes sharp and steady.

"Ready for ignition," Dean called back.

"Do it," Sloan commanded.

The lab went dead silent. Every engineer stepped back behind the reinforced blast shields, holding their breath.

Gregson’s hands trembled slightly as he hovered over the main ignition console. He looked at Dean. Dean gave a single, firm nod.

"Ignition sequence initiated," Gregson muttered into the comms mic. "Three... two... one... engage."

Inside the vacuum chamber, the A9 core flared to life.

A brilliant, blinding flash of cobalt-blue plasma erupted within the magnetic containment ring. The high-pitched whine of the propulsion pumps surged upward, climbing past three thousand hertz... past thirty-five hundred... hitting four thousand hertz dead on.

In the past, this was the exact moment the outer casing would begin to shudder, the micro-fractures spreading like spiderwebs across the titanium wall before imminent structural collapse.

On the diagnostic monitors, the vibration line remained flat, smooth, and green.

The harmonic feedback loop was gone. The propulsion stream roared with absolute, flawless stability.

For five seconds, nobody breathed. Then, the diagnostic system chimed with a crisp, triumphant success tone.

Test cycle complete. Structural integrity: 100%.

The lab erupted into deafening cheers. Gregson slammed his hands down on the console, laughing hysterically in pure relief. Across the room, two junior engineers high-fived.

On the observation platform, Sloan Hutton stared at the monitor, a slow, triumphant smile spreading across her face. She set her coffee cup down and walked toward the stairs.
