At 3:00 a.m., the celebration in the lab had quieted down. The engineers had dispersed to their quarters to prepare for the morning procurement review, leaving only the low, steady hum of the operational systems in the background.

Dean stood alone by the blast-resistant glass, wiping down his toolbox.

Footsteps clicked softly behind him.

"You're a hard man to pin down, Mr. Miller," Sloan’s voice broke the quiet. She walked up beside him, holding a manila folder and a crisp corporate check. "I had my compliance team run a secondary background check on you while you were rebuilding the manifold."

Dean didn't stop wiping his wrench. "Must have been a short report."

"It was," Sloan said, her voice dropping its usual executive edge, carrying a quiet, probing curiosity. "Because for the last three years, Dean Miller doesn't exist in the corporate databases. But before that? Dr. Dean Miller was the principal director of advanced propulsion research at AeroCore Dynamics."

Dean’s hand froze on the metal wrench.

He didn't look at her.

"AeroCore," Sloan continued softly, opening the folder to reveal a printed research paper with his face smiling on the author bio page from five years ago. "You held twelve patents. You designed the original plasma-shearing logic that every major aerospace firm in the country has been trying to reverse-engineer for a decade. Why did you walk away? Why are you cleaning air vents in my building?"

The silence stretched between them, heavy and suffocating.

Dean slowly set the wrench down inside his toolbox and latched the metal clasps with a sharp click.

"AeroCore was bought out by a larger conglomerate," Dean said, his voice flat, devoid of emotion, like a man reciting a gravestone inscription. "They wanted to cut corners on safety protocols to rush a commercial flight contract to market. I refused to sign off on the structural stress tests because the composite materials were going to fail under high-altitude thermal shock."

He turned his head to look at Sloan, his eyes cold and dark.

"They forged my signature on the compliance documents anyway. When I threatened to blow the whistle, they buried me in retaliatory litigation, drained my patents through corporate loopholes, and blackballed me from every engineering firm in North America. By the time the dust settled, my savings were gone, my wife had left, and I was staring at eviction notices."

Sloan stood motionless, the manila folder slipping slightly in her grip.

"So you dropped off the grid," she whispered.

"I didn't drop off the grid. I went underground where nobody could find me," Dean said, his voice hardening. "Because I had someone who depended on me. Someone who couldn't survive if I spent my time fighting corporate lawyers in court."

Sloan looked down at the check in her hand—the fifty thousand dollars he had requested.

"This isn't just for a bonus, is it?" she asked quietly.

Dean reached out, took the check from her fingers, and slid it into his breast pocket.

"This is for my daughter's heart surgery," Dean said. He picked up his toolbox, slung it over his shoulder, and turned toward the exit. "Thank you for the prompt payment, Ms. Hutton. You’ll find your air vents are clean for the next six months."
