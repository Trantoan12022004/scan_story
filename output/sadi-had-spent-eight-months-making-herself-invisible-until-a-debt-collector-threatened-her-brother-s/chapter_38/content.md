London greeted us three days later with a cold, damp mist that clung to the stone embankments of the Thames like a shroud. The city was a sprawling, ancient beast of finance, tradition, and hidden daggers, but it knelt just as easily as New York when the Castello name was whispered in the upper echelons of the City's financial district.

Our private Maybach glided silently past the Houses of Parliament, its tinted windows shielding us from the gray, drizzly drizzle of the afternoon. In the back seat, I reviewed the financial dossiers of Sterling Holdings while Victor monitored real-time satellite feeds of our maritime terminals across the Channel.

"Lord Sterling has called an emergency dinner at his private club in St. James’s," I said without looking up from my tablet. "He thinks an intimate setting will allow him to appeal to personal relationships rather than corporate ledger sheets."

"Sentimentality is the last refuge of a dying aristocracy," Victor remarked coldly, his eyes reflecting the blue light of his device. "He's hoping to invoke old school-tie networks and social etiquette to negotiate a discount on the Channel logistics fee."

"He'll find our etiquette strictly transactional," I replied, locking the screen and setting the tablet aside as the car slowed to a smooth halt before an imposing, ivy-covered brick townhouse with wrought-iron gas lamps.

The interior of the St. James’s club smelled of aged scotch, polished mahogany, and centuries of inherited privilege. Lord Sterling—a weathered man in his late sixties with thinning white hair and a tailored tweed suit that cost more than a small car—rose from a leather wingback chair by a crackling fireplace as we were ushered into the private dining parlor.

"Mr. Costello. Mrs. Costello," Sterling greeted, his voice carrying the clipped, upper-crust cadence of British nobility. But beneath the polite veneer, his eyes were darting nervously around the room, tracking our security detail as they quietly secured the perimeter. "Thank you for making the journey across the Atlantic on such short notice."

"We don't travel for emergencies, Lord Sterling," Victor said smoothly, not waiting for an invitation as he took a seat at the head of the table, gesturing for me to sit beside him. "We travel to close files. Let's dispense with the pleasantries. You requested this audience. State your terms."

Sterling swallowed hard, sinking back into his chair. He had expected arrogance, perhaps even aggression, but Victor’s chilling composure cut through the room like a scalpel.

"The Channel Tunnel transit fees your network has proposed... they're crippling," Sterling began, leaning forward and resting his elbows on the table. "My board believes that a twenty-five percent reduction across all regional freight lines is a fair compromise for granting Castello exclusive access to the British rail grid."

I tilted my head, studying him with the detached curiosity of a scientist examining a specimen under a microscope. "Your board, Lord Sterling, is looking at a balance sheet that won't exist by Friday morning if you don't sign our terms."

Sterling’s face flushed a mottled red. "Are you threatening me in my own club, madam?"

"It's not a threat," I said softly, my voice carrying an absolute, unshakeable calm that made the old man freeze. "It's an operational forecast. As of nine o'clock this morning, Castello holdings acquired thirty-four percent of Sterling Shipping’s floating debt from your primary creditors in Zurich. By noon tomorrow, we convert that debt into voting shares. You won't have a board by the end of the week, Lord Sterling. You’ll have a retirement package and a very quiet exit interview."

Silence descended upon the parlor, broken only by the crackle of the fireplace and the steady patter of rain against the tall sash windows. Sterling stared at me, the last vestiges of his aristocratic bluster evaporating into the cold London air. He looked suddenly very old, very tired, and utterly defeated.
