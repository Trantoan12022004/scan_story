The harsh fluorescent lights of the precinct briefing room flickered as Sergeant Miller tossed a fresh set of incident reports onto the stainless-steel table. It was 1:30 PM on Saturday afternoon, and the air in the room was thick with the smell of stale coffee and bureaucratic tension.

“Mason Bradley skipped town less than an hour after posting bail,” Sergeant Miller said, running a hand over his stubbled jaw. “Our highway patrol cameras caught his Ford F-150 heading south on Interstate 65 toward Alabama around sunrise. By the time the bail revocation warrant was signed by the judge, he was already past the state line.”

I stood by the window, staring out at the parking lot where my truck was parked. Amy sat at the table next to Julian Vance, her arms folded across her chest, her expression unreadable.

“How is that possible?” I demanded, turning back to face the sergeant. “He was caught red-handed with stolen property—the original deed to my house, forged bank documents, and fraudulent power-of-attorney forms. How did a local magistrate let him walk out on bail at 4:00 AM?”

“Because,” Julian interjected calmly, adjusting his cuffs, “bail is a constitutional right unless the defendant is classified as an immediate flight risk before formal arraignment. And since the formal charges hadn’t been locked into the county mainframe due to a weekend administrative lag, the duty judge had no legal grounds to hold him without a signed prosecutor’s motion.”

“Which my mother’s high-priced defense attorney arranged while we were still sleeping,” I muttered, my fists clenched so tightly my knuckles turned white. “She’s sitting in a holding cell, but her puppet is still out there running loose with whatever cash they haven’t managed to freeze yet.”

“Not quite loose,” Julian corrected, pulling a small tablet from his briefcase and sliding it across the table toward Sergeant Miller. “While you and Amy were securing the safety deposit box at Capital Union, my team ran a digital trace on the unmonitored account Mason used to post bail. It didn't come from a personal checking account. It came from an escrow account tied to a commercial warehouse down by the Cumberland River.”

Sergeant Miller leaned over the tablet, scrolling through a series of corporate filings and property registry documents.

“The Riverdale Industrial Park,” Miller read aloud, his eyebrows rising. “Warehouse 14-B. Wait a minute... I know this property. It’s an old shipping depot that’s been abandoned for years. But according to these records... it was purchased three weeks ago under a dummy corporation called Cumberland Logistics.”

“And who owns Cumberland Logistics?” Amy asked quietly, her voice cutting through the heavy silence of the room.

Julian tapped the screen twice.

“Teresa Bradley,” Julian replied. “Or, more accurately, the Teresa Bradley Legacy Trust. That warehouse is where your mother has been storing physical records, duplicate financial assets, and—according to an anonymous tip my firm received twenty minutes ago—significant cash reserves skimmed from your family accounts over the last three years.”

I stared at Julian, the pieces of the puzzle snapping together with terrifying clarity. The party last night wasn't just a celebration of control; it was a distraction. While my mother was playing the role of the grand matriarch humiliating my wife in our kitchen, Mason was supposed to be cleaning out Warehouse 14-B before the authorities caught wind of the investigation.

And now that Mason was on the run, heading south with whatever cash he could grab from that warehouse... he was going to destroy every shred of physical evidence that could link my mother to decades of systematic financial destruction.

“If Mason reaches that warehouse before we do, those ledgers, those cash reserves, and those backup servers are going up in smoke,” I said, grabbing my jacket from the back of the chair. “We need to move. Now.”

“Hold on, Mr. Bradley,” Sergeant Miller warned, standing up and reaching for his duty belt. “You can’t just storm a commercial property with a civilian. If Mason is desperate, he’s dangerous. I’m dispatching two tactical units to seal off the industrial park.”

“By the time your tactical units navigate downtown Saturday traffic, Mason will have torched the place and crossed into Alabama,” I replied sharply, my tone leaving no room for argument. “I know those warehouses. I worked on the structural retrofitting of that entire river district three years ago. There’s a secondary access tunnel behind loading bay 12 that doesn’t show up on standard municipal blueprints. If you send squad cars roaring through the front gates, he’ll vanish through the back before you even clear the perimeter.”

Julian looked at Sergeant Miller, giving the veteran cop a subtle, significant nod.

“He’s right, Sergeant,” Julian said quietly. “The legal window to seize those assets in real-time is closing. If we wait for a standard warrant execution, the evidence walks out the door with him.”

Sergeant Miller stared at me for a long, hard second, weighing the protocol against the reality of a cornered criminal. Finally, he unclipped his radio from his shoulder strap and handed it to me.

“Take this frequency, Bradley,” Miller said grimly. “Set it to tactical channel four. My units will approach from the perimeter and wait for your signal before breaching the main entrance. But if you do anything reckless, I’ll have you arrested right alongside your cousin. Understood?”

“Understood,” I said, taking the radio.

I looked at Amy, who had already stood up and grabbed her trench coat.

“You’re staying here,” I told her firmly.

“No, I’m not,” Amy replied, her voice leaving zero room for negotiation. “She tried to steal my home, my child, and my dignity. I am not sitting in a police precinct while you go hunting for her ghosts alone.”

I didn't argue. I knew that look in her eyes. It was the same look of fierce, unyielding survival I had seen when she stood up to my mother’s tyranny in the kitchen.

We walked out of the precinct together, climbed into my truck, and pointed the hood toward the Cumberland River.
