The conference room on the fifth floor of Sarah Vance’s law offices smelled of heavy paper, floor wax, and cold roasted coffee from the glass pot in the corner. Outside the wall-to-wall windows, an overcast afternoon hung low over the brick facade of the county administration building, casting a flat, gray light across the polished oak table.

Sarah Vance sat at the head of the table, her sleeves rolled up to her forearms, a thick red binder open before her. Her reading glasses rested low on the bridge of her nose as she checked off items on a typewritten checklist. Across from her, Elena sat with her shoulders straight, her hands folded quietly over a legal pad. Her skin had recovered much of its natural color during the weeks at the recovery center, though faint shadows still lingered beneath her eyes—quiet reminders of the months spent behind bolted doors.

"We go over your timeline one more time, Elena," Sarah said, her voice even and devoid of any theatricality. "Not because I doubt your memory, but because Richard’s counsel is going to try to rattle you on the stand with dates, medication logs, and the exact sequence of your discharge papers from St. Jude’s. They want to make it look like a voluntary retreat rather than a forced confinement."

Elena swallowed, her fingers tightening slightly against the paper. She looked up, her gaze steady. "It wasn't voluntary, Sarah. I signed nothing. They took my phone the second we arrived, and Richard told the intake supervisor I was suffering from a acute psychotic break. I remember the smell of the hallway on the third floor. I remember the key turning from the outside."

"Good," Sarah said, making a sharp checkmark in the margin. "Keep that exact concrete detail. Juries don't connect with legal definitions; they connect with keys turning from the outside."

I sat in a padded chair near the corner of the room, my arms crossed over my dark overcoat, watching the way Elena held her ground. Every time she spoke of those weeks, a cold knot tightened in my chest—the same knot I’d felt standing in that bright modern hallway with the heavy iron chain and the brass padlock clattering against the basement door. We had come a long way from that chaotic afternoon, but the proximity to the legal reckoning brought a different kind of tension, quiet and relentless.

"And my deposition?" I asked, shifting my weight.

Sarah turned a page, sliding a thick set of stapled sheets across the table toward me. "Your statement regarding the raid is ironclad, Marcus. The tactical team's body-cam footage corroborates your exact physical position, the state of the door, and Richard’s initial refusal to unlock it. The state’s attorney has already reviewed your affidavit. Richard’s legal team tried to file a motion to suppress your testimony on the grounds of trespassing and illegal entry, but the judge threw it out this morning within three minutes."

I picked up the pen resting beside the papers, turning it over between my fingers. "What about his financial accounts? The forensic accountant finished tracing those off-shore transfers linked to the charity foundation."

"That’s the final nail," Sarah said, a faint, humorless smile touching the corner of her mouth. "Richard didn't just misuse foundation funds to pay for the private psychiatric facility's hush-money invoices; he forged Elena’s signature on the asset transfer forms while she was locked away. It crosses the line from a nasty domestic dispute into federal wire fraud and kidnapping. His high-priced defense attorneys know it, too. They’ve been calling my office all morning trying to negotiate a plea deal that avoids a public trial."

Elena looked up sharply, her eyes widening. "A plea? What kind of plea?"

"Guilty to unlawful restraint and falsification of documents in exchange for a recommended sentence that still leaves room for parole in five years," Sarah said bluntly. "They want to protect his name, his country club memberships, and whatever scraps of his public image are left standing."

The room went still. The only sound was the low hum of the ceiling vent and the distant sound of traffic rising from the street below. Elena turned her head slightly, looking out the tall window at the gray sky. She didn't look angry; she looked tired, but resolute.

"No," Elena said softly. Her voice was barely above a whisper, but it carried absolute clarity. "If he takes a plea, it stays behind closed doors. People will still wonder. They’ll still whisper that maybe I was unstable, that maybe he was just a desperate husband trying to save his family. I want the judge and a jury to hear the whole thing. I want the locks, the chain, and the foundation accounts entered into public record."

Sarah studied Elena for a long, quiet moment, then slowly nodded. "Then we reject the plea by four o’clock this afternoon. We go to trial."

Sarah pushed a final document across the table toward me—the witness deposition confirming my signature and my personal account of the breach. I pulled it toward me, read the final paragraph, and signed my name at the bottom with firm, deliberate strokes, the ink dark against the white page.

"The trial date is set for Monday morning at nine sharp," Sarah said, closing the red binder with a sharp slap that echoed in the quiet room. "Get some rest this weekend, both of you. Monday is going to be a very long day."
