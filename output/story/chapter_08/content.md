The gravel in the parking lot of the Pine Ridge Recovery Center crunched under the tires of the sedan like dry bone. It was an overcast Tuesday afternoon, the kind of gray, sunless day that made the surrounding Virginia pine forests look flat and slate-colored. According to the ledgers I’d pulled from Richard’s foundation office, this was where the checks went—monthly wire transfers disguised as architectural consulting fees, routed through a shell company in Delaware, landing in the operating account of a private psychiatric facility tucked thirty miles off the interstate.

I cut the engine, the cooling fan ticking softly beneath the hood. My hands gripped the steering wheel, the skin across my knuckles still tight from the drive.

The main building was a single-story brick structure with tinted windows and manicured hedges that looked too stiff to be natural. A brass plaque by the double glass doors read *Pine Ridge Recovery Center: Behavioral Health & Wellness*. It looked quiet, too quiet, like an office park on a Sunday morning. But the security cameras mounted at the corners of the roof were modern, pivoting slowly on motorized bases.

I adjusted the collar of my dark coat, stepped out into the damp chill, and walked up the concrete steps. Inside, the lobby smelled faintly of lemon disinfectant and old carpet. A receptionist with silver hair styled into a tight bun sat behind a curved laminate desk, her eyes fixed on a computer screen. Behind her, a wall of monitors flickered with hallway views.

"Can I help you?" she asked without looking up, her fingers clicking rhythmically on her keyboard.

"I’m here to see Elena Vance," I said, keeping my voice flat and even. I didn't use her married surname.

The typing stopped. The receptionist looked up, her expression guarded behind wire-rimmed glasses. "Visiting hours are Tuesdays and Thursdays, two to four. Today is Wednesday."

"It's an urgent family matter," I said, taking a slow step closer to the desk. "Her brother is Sheriff Vance. He sent me to check on her paperwork."

The woman’s eyes narrowed slightly. She didn't blink. "We don't coordinate family visits or paperwork without prior authorization from the attending physician, Dr. Hayes, and an appointment booked forty-eight hours in advance. What did you say your name was?"

"Marcus," I said. "I'm working with the family."

She reached down, her hand disappearing briefly beneath the counter, likely resting on a silent alarm. "Mr. Marcus, I'm going to have to ask you to leave the premises. This is a private medical facility. If you have administrative inquiries, your attorney can contact our legal department during regular business hours."

She wasn't going to budge. The institutional brick wall was as solid as the one Richard had built around his house. I gave her a curt nod, turned on my heel, and walked back out the glass doors.

Instead of getting into the car, I kept walking along the paved perimeter of the building, keeping my hands deep in my pockets. The staff parking lot was situated at the rear, flanked by metal dumpsters and a set of heavy service doors marked *Authorized Personnel Only*.

I leaned against the fender of a faded station wagon, watching the shift change. Two nurses in blue scrubs walked out, laughing quietly about something on a smartphone, pushing open the heavy metal door. It didn't latch fully behind them; the rubber gasket caught on a pebble.

Moving casually, I crossed the asphalt and caught the edge of the door before it clicked shut.

Inside was a narrow utility corridor smelling of bleach and floor wax. Pipes ran along the high ceiling, wrapped in gray fiberglass insulation. I kept my head down, walking past a laundry staging area and a set of double doors labeled *Administration*. Faint sounds echoed from somewhere down the hall—the tinty rattle of a television, the squeak of rubber soles on linoleum.

I followed the corridor as it curved toward the residential wing. The doors here had small wired-glass windows set into the wood. Through one, I saw a sterile dayroom with bolted-down plastic chairs and a bolted table.

Then I heard her voice. It wasn't loud, just a low, rhythmic cadence speaking to someone who wasn't answering.

I stopped outside Room 112. The glass was narrow, but I could see inside. The room was small, containing only a metal-framed bed bolted to the floor and a chest of drawers with rounded corners. Standing by the window, her hands pressed against the cold glass, was a woman in a gray cotton tunic. Her hair, once a bright, familiar gold like Lily's, was streaked with silver and cropped short.

"Elena," I breathed, my voice barely audible over the hum of the overhead fluorescent light.

She turned her head slowly at the sound, her hollow eyes scanning the corridor. For a fraction of a second, our eyes locked through the wired glass. Her lips parted, recognition flickering across her gaunt face like a dying match.

Before she could speak, heavy footsteps echoed behind me.

"Hey! You can't be back here!"

I spun around. A broad-shouldered man in a gray security uniform was jogging down the corridor, his hand resting on the radio clipped to his belt.

"I took a wrong turn looking for the exit," I said, holding my hands up openly, palms out.

"This is a restricted wing. Turn around right now or I'm calling the county deputies," the guard barked, stepping into my personal space, his breath smelling of stale coffee.

"I'm leaving," I said, keeping my voice steady as I backed toward the service doors.

The guard escorted me all the way across the back lot, watching as I climbed into my sedan and started the engine. He stood by the dumpsters, arms crossed, jotting down my license plate number in a small yellow notepad as I shifted into drive. I didn't rush. I pulled out onto the gravel road, checked my rearview mirror, and watched the pine trees close in around the gate.
