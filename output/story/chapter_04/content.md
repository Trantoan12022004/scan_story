The neon sign outside the diner window flickered with a tired, rhythmic buzz, casting a dull red glow across the Formica table top. It was past midnight, and State Route Four was dead quiet except for the occasional hiss of an eighteen-wheeler downshifting on the interstate bypass a mile over. I stared down at a ceramic mug of black coffee, watching the dark liquid ripple every time a heavy truck rattled the window frames.

My knuckles were still bruised. The skin across the side of my right hand throbbed where it had connected with Richard’s cheek hours ago in that pristine, terrifyingly bright hallway. I could still hear Lily’s small, frantic voice echoing in my head, and the heavy thud of the tactical officers' boots as they swarmed the house. But the adrenaline had finally burned out, leaving behind a cold, hollow weight in my chest.

I pulled a small, dog-eared notebook from my dark overcoat pocket. The pages were filled with scribbled dates, names of neighbors who had heard muffled shouting through the shared party walls, and unanswered messages I’d left over the past six months when Elena had first vanished from school pickups and Sunday dinners. Everyone had told me to mind my own business. The local property association down the street had filed my complaints away under domestic disputes, treating a mother’s sudden disappearance and a locked basement door as private family matters.

"Refill?"

The waitress standing beside the booth had tired eyes and a stained apron, holding a glass decanter of lukewarm water and coffee.

"No, I'm good, thanks," I said, not looking up from the notebook.

She lingered for a second, her gaze dropping to my bruised hand before she moved down the counter toward a truck driver reading a tattered newspaper.

My phone vibrated sharply against the table, the metallic rattle breaking the hum of the old ceiling fan. I snatched it up before the second ring.

"Marcus," Social Worker Miller’s voice came through the speaker, crisp and exhausted. "I'm updating from the county intake office."

"How is Lily?" I asked, sitting up straight.

"She's settled into the temporary placement facility downtown. It's quiet, she has her own room, and a pediatric nurse checked her over. No physical injuries beyond malnourishment and exhaustion, but she’s refusing to speak to anyone who isn't wearing a badge or asking about you." Miller paused, letting out a heavy breath. "Richard has already retained private counsel. He’s claiming you harassed him, forced your way into his residence, and coached the child."

"He locked her in a basement behind a chain and a padlock," I said, my voice dropping an octave, tight with controlled fury. "I was standing right there when the door came off."

"I saw the officer's report, Marcus. I know what was in that basement. But Richard is connected in this county, and his foundation funds half the civic programs downtown. He’s going to use every legal lever available to get emergency custody back on Monday morning, claiming he was simply securing the house during a mental health episode." Miller’s tone grew stern. "If you don't have representation locked down by sunrise, you won't even be allowed in the courtroom, let alone get kinship standing."

"I'm handling it," I said.

"See that you do. I'll send the preliminary intake files to a secure drop. Don't do anything reckless, Marcus. The system is watching you just as hard as it's watching him now."

The line clicked dead.

I set the phone down and stared at the dark coffee. Miller was right. Anger wasn't going to hold up against a high-priced family law attorney backed by charitable donations and quiet county favors. I needed someone who didn't care about Richard’s board seats or his pristine white shirts.

I opened my contacts list and scrolled down to a number I had saved three years ago during a bitter property dispute for a neighbor who couldn't afford to lose her home. Sarah Vance didn't take high-profile charity cases for the press; she took cases that everyone else said were unwinnable.

I pressed dial, listening to the steady, rhythmic tone cut through the quiet diner. She picked up on the third ring, her voice sharp and completely alert, as if she had been sitting at her desk all night waiting for the call.

"This better be good, Marcus. It's half past midnight."

"Sarah, I need you on an emergency custody and concealment case," I said, looking out the rain-streaked window toward the empty highway. "We're in front of Judge Callahan on Monday morning, and I have every intention of tearing Richard’s life down to the foundation."

There was a short pause on the line, the sound of papers rustling in the background. Then, a dry, deliberate sigh.

"Send me the address and whatever police reports you managed to grab," Sarah said. "I'll meet you outside the county courthouse at seven a.m. sharp. Don't be late."
