The waiting room of the county sheriff's office smelled of instant coffee, wet wool, and floor wax. Outside the narrow wired-glass windows, a steady autumn rain blurred the flashing amber light of a parked cruiser. Lily sat in a heavy plastic chair beside me, her small legs swinging a few inches above the linoleum. She still wore the yellow dress, though the hem was damp and gray with mud from the basement stairs. Her small fingers clutched a paper cup of water I'd gotten from the machine down the hall. Every time the heavy outer door banged open against the wind, her shoulders jerked upward and her eyes darted to the latch.

"He's not coming in here, Lily," I said quietly, leaning down so my eyes met hers. "You're safe. We're right here with the sheriff."

She nodded once, a small, tight movement, but didn't speak. She hadn't let go of my sleeve since we'd climbed out of the patrol car.

Sheriff Vance pushed open the door to the inner offices, holding a thick clipboard against his flannel shirt. He looked tired, the gray stubble on his jaw looking heavier than it had back at the house. He glanced from Lily down to me, his expression flat.

"Marcus," Vance said, his voice dropping as he stepped into the room. "Social worker just pulled in. Name's Miller. She’s set up in Interview Room B. Wants to talk to the girl alone first, then with you."

I stood up, adjusting the collar of my dark overcoat. "She doesn't need to be grilled, Vance. She just spent an hour staring at a padlock."

"She’s a minor removed from a locked residence during an active domestic call, Marcus. Standard intake applies. You're lucky I didn't book you for that right hook you threw in the hallway before the deputies pulled you off him." Vance tapped the clipboard with a stubby thumb. "Speaking of which, your friend Richard lawyered up before he even cleared his front porch. High-priced firm out of the county seat. They're already filing motions for unlawful detention and assault."

"Let them file," I said, my jaw tightening. "Ask Miller if I can sit in the corner of that room. She won't talk to a stranger without someone she recognizes."

Vance studied me for a long second, then let out a slow breath through his nose. "Ten minutes. Then Miller takes over the session properly. Come on."

Interview Room B was small, dominated by a scarred metal table and three bolted chairs. The walls were painted a dull institutional beige, marred by scuff marks near the floor. Social Worker Miller was a woman in her late forties with sharp wire-rimmed glasses and a gray wool blazer. She was already organizing a stack of carbon-copy forms when we walked in.

"Sit down, Lily," Miller said, her tone professional but not unkind. She looked at me over her frames. "Mr. Vance said you could stay for the preliminary, but no coaching. I need her own words."

Lily kept her hand firmly tucked into my coat pocket as she slid into the chair. I sat beside her, keeping my hands flat on the cold metal surface of the table.

"Lily, do you know why you're here at the sheriff's station?" Miller asked, clicking a ballpoint pen.

Lily stared down at her lap. "Because daddy put the chain on."

"What chain, sweetie?"

"The big one. For the basement." Lily’s voice was barely a whisper, but it cut cleanly through the hum of the fluorescent overhead ballast. "Mommy was down there first. Then he told me to go to my room, but I heard them yelling. When mommy didn't come up, he locked the door. He said nobody was allowed to open it."

Miller's pen moved steadily across the paper. "How long was your mother down there, Lily?"

"I don't know," the girl murmured, her lower lip trembling. "Days, I think. She wouldn't answer when I knocked on the wood."

My chest tightened, the memory of that heavy iron chain burning fresh behind my eyes. I shifted in my seat, but Miller raised a single finger to warn me against interrupting.

Before Miller could ask the next question, the frosted glass door of the station's outer corridor swung open with a sharp click. Heavy footsteps echoed down the linoleum hallway, far sharper and faster than the deputies' usual tread. The door to Interview Room B pushed open without a knock.

Richard stood in the threshold. He had changed out of the torn shirt from the house into a crisp, charcoal-gray suit, though a faint, purplish bruise still mottled the pale skin of his left cheek where my knuckles had connected. Flanking him was a tall, thin man in a bespoke navy suit carrying a leather briefcase—counsel from the county seat.

"This is completely unauthorized," Richard's lawyer said, his voice smooth and carrying the practiced cadence of a courtroom regular. "My client has not surrendered custody, and this minor is being questioned without legal representation present."

Sheriff Vance appeared behind them in the doorway, looking thoroughly unimpressed. "Hold on right there, Counselor. This is an active child welfare intake following a sheriff's department entry under exigent circumstances. You wait in the lobby."

"We'll see what the duty judge says about exigent circumstances at midnight," Richard said, his eyes fixing on Lily with a cold, terrifying calm that the social worker and the lawyer missed entirely. He didn't look at me at all. He didn't need to. "Lily. You're coming home with me now."

Lily shrank back against my shoulder, her fingers gripping the fabric of my coat so hard my knuckles ached.

"She's not going anywhere with you, Richard," I said, standing up slowly from the metal chair.

Miller stepped between us, her pen still clutched in her hand. "Gentlemen. Both of you. This room is under my jurisdiction right now. Mr. Richard, step outside immediately or I will have the deputies remove you."

Richard's gaze flicked to Miller, then back to Lily. A faint, razor-thin smile touched the corner of his mouth—the public face of a man who owned half the county's charity boards and believed every system could be bought or bluffed.

"We'll let the court decide who the unstable one is," Richard said smoothly, turning on his heel. "Come along, Counsel. We have emergency injunctions to file."

As the door clicked shut behind them, Miller let out a long breath and dropped her pen onto the forms. She looked at Lily, then up at me with an expression that mixed professional duty with quiet grimness.

"He's right about one thing," Miller said softly. "The emergency hearing is at eight tomorrow morning. And until then... I can't leave her here, and I can't give her to him."

"Where is she going?" I asked.

"A vetted emergency foster home three towns over," Miller said, pulling a pink carbon sheet from her clipboard. "It's temporary, Marcus. Just until the judge hears the protective custody motion. But you won't be on the paperwork as kinship. Not yet."

I looked down at Lily. Her eyes were wide, blinking back tears she refused to let fall. I reached out and gently brushed a strand of blonde hair away from her forehead.

"I'll be right behind you," I told her, my voice low and steady. "Every mile of the way."
