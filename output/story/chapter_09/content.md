The fluorescent lights of Sarah Vance’s office hummed with a flat, persistent vibration that cut through the silence of the empty building. Outside the frosted glass door, the downtown street was quiet, save for the occasional hiss of tires on the wet asphalt. I dropped the folder of photographs and handwritten timestamps onto Sarah’s desk, the heavy manila stock sliding across the polished wood.

Sarah looked up from a stack of financial printouts, her reading glasses slipping slightly down the bridge of her nose. She didn’t touch the folder immediately. Instead, she leaned back in her leather chair, studying my face—the dark circles under my eyes, the set of my jaw, the physical toll of the last forty-eight hours.

"You went back out there," she said, her voice low and steady. It wasn't a question.

"I had to confirm it," I said, pulling out the chair opposite her and sitting down. "Pine Ridge isn't a treatment center. It's a holding facility. They've got Elena listed under a maiden name that hasn't existed in ten years. No visitors allowed without Richard's direct sign-off. If we wait for standard discovery motions next week, he'll move her again before the judge even signs the subpoena."

Sarah crossed her arms, leaning forward over the desk. "Marcus, listen to me. If you take matters into your own hands—if you try to pull her out of there yourself—you’ll hand Richard everything he needs on a silver platter. He wants you to look like a rogue vigilante. He’s counting on you losing your temper again so he can get a permanent restraining order and lock down the guardianship of both of them."

"I'm not talking about breaking in," I said, rubbing the back of my neck where the muscle felt like a coiled spring. "I'm talking about the law. You told me yourself that a writ of habeas corpus bypasses the normal waiting periods if there's imminent danger of unlawful detention. We have the timestamps. We have the facility logs I pulled from the delivery registry. And we have the financial trail showing Richard funneling foundation funds into private psychiatric retainers that don't match any medical billing code."

She reached out and finally pulled the folder toward her. She flipped it open, her eyes scanning the grainy photographs of the facility's back entrance, the license plates of the administrative staff, and the printed bank routing numbers I'd stayed up half the night cross-referencing. The silence in the room stretched out, broken only by the hum of the overhead lights and the ticking of a small brass clock on the bookshelf.

After a long minute, Sarah closed the folder with a soft thud.

"This is enough for an emergency writ," she admitted, looking up at me. "It’s aggressive, and a judge is going to look twice at a petition filed at two in the morning, but the paper trail on those funds is clean. It shows intent to conceal." She reached for her desk phone and punched a direct extension without taking her eyes off mine. "Let's get Sheriff Vance on the line. If we're going to execute this writ at dawn, we need county backup, not local township police who play golf with Richard on Sundays."

The phone rang twice before a gravelly voice answered on the other end. Sarah didn't waste time with pleasantties.

"Sheriff, sorry to wake you. I'm looking at verified documentation that Richard Vance is holding a witness and legal guardian in an unlisted private facility under false administrative pretenses. We're filing an emergency writ of habeas corpus within the hour... Yes, right now. I need your deputies at the gates of Pine Ridge by six a.m. sharp to secure the perimeter while we serve it."

I listened to the low murmur of the sheriff's response through the receiver, catching the curt affirmative before Sarah clicked the phone back into its cradle. She looked across the desk at me, her expression grim but resolute.

"He's in," Sarah said. "The sheriff doesn't like Richard's influence in the county any more than we do. But once those cruiser lights flash at the gates, there’s no turning back. Richard will throw every dollar and every high-priced attorney he has at us."

"Let him try," I said, standing up as the first pale streaks of gray light began to bleed through the blinds of the office window. Dawn was coming, and with it, the end of the quiet.
